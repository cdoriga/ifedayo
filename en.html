A crest of many cultures

Ìfẹ́dayọ̀ tells what a guardian's crest is for, and why nobody fits into one single picture.

Read the story

Do you know why every guardian has a crest? Among the K-pop hunters, each one has her own colour, her own symbol and her own way of fighting, and that's how we recognise each of them on stage and in the fight against the shadows. Crests were invented by the knights of long ago: in armour and helmets, nobody could see anybody's face, so each family painted a design on its shield. A guardian's crest shows who she is on the inside.

But I learned something: a person doesn't fit into one single picture. Look at me. I was born in Minas Gerais, in Brazil, and my name comes from Yoruba, the language of my ancestors, across the sea. I sing K-pop, which comes from Korea, mixed with afrobeat, which was born in Nigeria. I fight with capoeira, which Black people created in Brazil, and with ballet, which comes from Europe. And all of that is me, the whole of me.

Brazil is like that too. Indigenous peoples lived there long before everyone else, and their languages gave us words you use too: jaguar, piranha, tapioca and cashew. Many peoples came from Africa: the Yoruba, who in Brazil are also called Nagô, and the peoples of Congo and Angola. With them came the drum, samba, congado, and sweet words like cafuné, a gentle stroke through someone's hair. And the Portuguese brought the language people speak in Brazil.

Later, lots of people crossed the sea to live in Brazil. Whole families came from Italy to work the land and grow grapes, mostly in the south of the country. Many people came from Germany too. Families came from Japan to grow coffee and vegetables, and today Brazil has more people of Japanese descent than any other country outside Japan. Families from Syria and Lebanon opened shops all over Brazil, and that's how esfiha and kibbeh became Brazilian bakery food.

This story wasn't always fair. Many African people were brought to Brazil by force, and Indigenous peoples lost much of their land. This mix had a price, and the ones who paid the most were them, who often had no choice. That's why we remember this story with respect, and that's why no culture is worth more than another.

And pão de queijo, the cheese bread from Minas? Its flour, polvilho, comes from cassava, which Indigenous people taught others to grow. The cheese came with the Portuguese. And it was the cooks of Minas, many of them Black women, who put it all together. One bite and there you go: three cultures in one little ball!

It's this whole mix that makes Brazilian people so beautiful. A person can be made of many cultures at once, and each one adds to the others.

So when you build your crest, you can mix as much as you like: a Yoruba leather shield, adire cloth, a medieval queen's colour, K-pop sparkle and an animal from Brazil, like the capybara or the caracara. Now tell me: which cultures live inside you? And where does your family come from?

When someone gives you a funny look

A talk with Ìfẹ́dayọ̀ about what you might feel, what to do and who is on your side.

Read the talk

Let me tell you something that happened to me. One day I was in a shop with my mum, and the shop assistant started following us from one side to the other, giving us funny looks, as if we were about to do something wrong. He didn't do that to the other people. It was because of the colour of our skin. That has a name: racism.

I felt lots of things all at once: sadness, anger, shame, a lump in my throat. It's okay to feel that. And I learned something important: it's never the fault of the person who is treated badly. The problem is the prejudice of the person giving the funny looks.

Sometimes we see someone we love being treated in a way we aren't. It feels heavy in your chest, and sometimes you even feel a strange kind of guilt. That guilt isn't yours. If it shows up, tell a grown-up you trust.

Do you know what I did? I stayed close to my mum, I held her hand, and afterwards we talked about everything. If one day you see someone treating you, someone in your family or anyone at all badly, you don't have to fix it by yourself. Go to a grown-up you trust and tell them what you saw and what you felt. Telling is brave.

In the same family there are people with darker skin and lighter skin, with kinky, curly or straight hair, and every one of them belongs to the family completely. Your skin is yours, and your family's story lives in you. And you have a beautiful power: to stand by the person who was treated badly, take their hand and tell a grown-up.

There's always someone with you: the people who love you, even from far away, and me, with my drum. When the drum plays, nobody is left on their own, remember?

Ìfẹ́dayọ̀'s drum

Why the drum is her instrument, and what it means to so many peoples around the world.

Did you know that the first drum we ever hear is our mother's heart? While still in the tummy, a baby can already hear it: thump-thump, thump-thump. Maybe that's why everyone is born knowing a little bit of rhythm.

Why the drum is my instrument

▶ Play the Ìfẹ́dayọ̀'s Drum playlist on Spotify

My drum is related to the dùndún, the Yoruba talking drum. Yoruba is a language where the voice goes up and down in tone, and the dùndún copies that up and down. Long ago it carried messages from one village to another, and even today it speaks names and proverbs at celebrations.

When many African people were brought to Brazil by force, they couldn't bring almost anything. But they brought their rhythms, kept in their memory and in their hands. That's how drums were born again in Brazil: in candomblé, in the congado of Minas, in samba, in maracatu and in capoeira.

In candomblé, an Afro-Brazilian religion, the drums called atabaques are sacred, and each rhythm is a way of greeting and calling the orixás.

And there's more: to play the drum you don't need to read music, just your hands and your heart. The drum calls everyone close, to dance and sing together. That's why it's my weapon against the shadows: when the drum plays, nobody is left on their own.

Drums around the world

Almost everywhere in the world there's a drum. In Japan, the taiko are so big you feel the sound in your chest. In India, the tabla has two little drums that talk really fast. In Korea there's the buk, which plays with the others in samulnori.

For many Indigenous peoples of the Americas, the drum is like the heartbeat of the Earth. And in the north of Portugal, at street festivals, the Zés Pereiras go by playing enormous bass drums. If you ever hear one, you know what to do: stamp your feet and dance!

How to get to know a country

Do you know what you can do when you want to get to know a new country? You start by studying. You study the country and its whole region, to understand how people live there.

You read about its history, watch films made by people from there, listen to its music and taste its food. The playlists in this book were born that way.

I think that's beautiful. Every people knows how to tell its own story, and music is one of the loveliest ways to listen. Whoever listens carefully learns to respect people just as they are.

Borrowing with respect

When we love another people's music, dance or clothes, we can learn, sing along and dance along. Music is made to be shared! But we need to be careful. Sometimes someone takes something from another people, uses it as a costume or a joke and forgets who created it. Or they become famous and make money from it, while the people who created it stay forgotten. That has a name: cultural appropriation.

In Brazil, samba and capoeira, created by Black people, were once banned. Later they became the pride of the whole country, and many people forgot where they came from. And an Indigenous feather headdress isn't a carnival costume: for many Indigenous peoples it has an important meaning.

So before I use something from another culture, I ask myself a few questions. Where does it come from? Who created it? Is it sacred to someone? I sing K-pop myself, which comes from Korea, very far from my homeland. That's why I study, listen to people from there and always say where it comes from. Borrowing with respect means this: saying the name of whoever created it, with love and with pride.

Afrobeat, the drum that talks

▶ Play Ìfẹ́dayọ̀'s playlist on Spotify

Afrobeat was born in Lagos, a huge city in Nigeria, at the end of the 1960s. It was created by the musician Fela Kuti, together with the drummer Tony Allen. They mixed Yoruba music, highlife from Ghana, and the jazz and funk of Black musicians in the United States.

The songs were long, with lots of drums, lots of horns and a choir answering the singer. And Fela sang about the injustices he saw, so people would think about them together. Today singers from Nigeria and Ghana make afrobeats, with an s at the end, the younger cousin that gets the whole world dancing.

And my drum? It's related to the dùndún, the Yoruba talking drum. Because Yoruba is a language where the voice goes up and down, almost like a song, the talking drum can copy words. It really talks!

K-pop + Afrobeat

K-pop, practice and mixing

▶ Play Ìfẹ́dayọ̀'s K-pop playlist on Spotify

K-pop comes from South Korea, a country in Asia. In 1992, a group called Seo Taiji and Boys mixed rap and hip-hop with Korean music, and many people say that's where today's K-pop began.

The groups train for many years to sing and dance perfectly together, everyone in step. And K-pop is a mix too: the hip-hop and R&B inside it were created by Black people in the United States.

Korea has its own ancient drums too. In samulnori, four percussion instruments play together, and each one stands for something in the sky: thunder, wind, rain and cloud. I sing K-pop with afrobeat because that's who I am, a mix.

Brasil

Brazilian music, a circle of many peoples

▶ Play the Brazil playlist on Spotify

The music of Brazil is made of many peoples. Indigenous peoples were already singing and dancing there with the maracá and the flute. African peoples brought drums and singing in a circle, with call and response. The Portuguese brought the viola and the cavaquinho, a little guitar.

In Rio de Janeiro, musicians used to meet at the house of Tia Ciata, a candomblé priestess who came from Bahia. That's where Pelo Telefone came from, one of the first sambas ever recorded, more than a hundred years ago. In the Northeast, the accordion, the zabumba drum and the triangle became forró, with Luiz Gonzaga. And in Minas there's congado, with queens, kings and drums.

Then came bossa nova, axé, funk, rap and so much more. Every Brazilian rhythm keeps the story of lots of people, including those who had to fight hard just to be able to play and dance.

Asia

Asia, gongs and drums

▶ Play the Asia playlist on Spotify

Asia is the biggest continent in the world. In Indonesia, on the island of Bali, gamelan orchestras play gongs and bronze keys. Everyone has to listen to each other really well for the music to come out whole.

In Japan there are the taiko, huge drums you feel in your chest. In the Philippines there's the kulintang, a row of little gongs played with two sticks. And in Korea, you already know: there's K-pop! Every country has its own language, its own food and its own music.

Latin America

Latin America, we belong here too

▶ Play the Latin playlist on Spotify

Brazil speaks Portuguese and almost all the countries nearby speak Spanish, but they're all part of the same Latin America. They have lots in common: the mix of Indigenous, African and European peoples, street festivals, food made from corn and loads of drumming.

And music goes back and forth across the border all the time. In the south of Brazil, the music has a lot of Argentine rock and cumbia, and also milonga and chamamé, which are played on both sides of the border. In Minas, my homeland, Milton Nascimento sang together with the Argentine singer Mercedes Sosa. In Pará, lambada was born by mixing carimbó with cumbia and merengue. And samba and bossa nova are played all over Latin America.

In Cuba, son was born, and it helped create salsa. There's tango from Argentina and Uruguay, Andean music with bamboo flutes and reggaeton, which gets everybody dancing. One teaches the other, and everyone ends up dancing.

Italy

Italy, the songs that crossed the sea

▶ Play the Italy playlist on Spotify

Did you know that more than a hundred years ago, lots and lots of Italian families sailed to Brazil? They crossed the sea and took their songs to the south of Brazil. One of them is called Mérica, Mérica, and it tells the story of the emigrants' journey.

Even today, in the south of Brazil, some people sing this song and speak Talian, a language that comes from northern Italy, mostly from the Veneto region, and mixed with Portuguese. And it was in Italy that opera was born, where the whole story is told by singing. Just the way I like it!

Cumbia and the keyboard battle

Cumbia was born on the coast of Colombia, near the Caribbean Sea. It brings together the gaita, an Indigenous flute, the drums of African peoples and the clothes and verses that came from Spain. The dancers wear long, swirling skirts, and long ago they danced holding lit candles.

Then cumbia travelled all over Latin America and took on a different style in each place. In Peru it got electric guitar and Andean melodies. In Mexico it plays at neighbourhood parties. In Argentina the keyboard became the star: it plays the punteos, those super-fast melodies everyone recognises.

And then they invented something really fun: the keyboard battle. Keyboard players face off one against one, and each plays their best solo. Then the audience votes online, and the winner goes through to the next round. In Argentina, in 2026, it even became a championship, the Mundial de Tecladistas. The first one was won by Tobi Valenzuela.

It's almost as if they were inspired by the K-pop hunters, right? They also use the keyboard and sound to compete. But they're not heroes, there are no monsters and nobody fights. They just want to play, show what they can do and have fun. And the biggest winners are the people dancing!

Opera, mermaids and the song that enchants

▶ Play the opera playlist on Spotify

Did you know that mermaids and singing have gone together for thousands of years? In almost every mermaid story, her greatest power is her voice.

In ancient Greece, people said the sirens lived on an island and sang so beautifully that sailors forgot everything and followed the music. The hero Odysseus wanted to hear them without being in danger: he asked his friends to block their ears with wax and tie him to the ship's mast. And do you know the strangest part? The first Greek sirens were half woman and half bird. The fish tail only came later, in stories from the Middle Ages.

In Brazil there's Iara, the mother of the river waters, who sings to call anyone walking along the bank. In many places in Africa and the Caribbean, people tell of Mami Wata, a water spirit often drawn with a fish tail. And in Brazil many people paint Iemanjá, the great mother of the sea, as a mermaid. In candomblé, Iemanjá is an orixá.

And opera? It was born in Italy, about four hundred years ago. It's a kind of theatre where the whole story is told by singing, with an orchestra, costumes and scenery. Opera singers train for many years, until their voice is so strong it fills an enormous theatre without any microphone. The highest voice of all is called soprano.

And there's a mermaid opera! A composer from the Czech Republic called Dvořák wrote Rusalka, the story of a water nymph who falls in love with a prince. One night she sings to the Moon and asks the Moon to tell the prince what she feels. It's one of the most beautiful songs in the world.

And opera has a link to Sintra too. Remember King Fernando, the one from the Pena Palace? He fell in love with an opera singer, Elise, when she was singing at the São Carlos Theatre in Lisbon. They got married, she became the Countess of Edla and designed a little hidden house in the Pena park, which is still called the Countess's Chalet today.

And there's a singer I really admire: Joaquina Lapinha. She was born in Brazil more than two hundred years ago, the daughter of a free Black woman, crossed the sea and sang opera at the São Carlos Theatre in Lisbon for ten years. She was probably the first Black singer from the Americas to sing on the stages of Europe. Many others came after her, like the Brazilian Bidu Sayão and the Americans Leontyne Price and Jessye Norman.

Now it's your turn to be a soprano: fill your tummy with air, open your mouth wide and sing one really long note. Ask someone to count how many seconds you can hold it!

Princesses of the world

Real and fairy-tale princesses and queens, from Portugal, Africa, Brazil, Asia and the Pacific.Stories of princesses and queens from many peoples, chosen because they act: they rule, study, choose and resist.

Princesses from all over the world

Did you know there are princesses all over the world? Every people has its own stories of princesses and queens. Some really lived, a long time ago. Others live in the tales that grandmothers still tell today.

My favourites are the brave ones: the ones who studied, who chose their own path and who defended their people. Come and meet them! There are princesses from Portugal, Africa, Brazil, Korea, Japan and some islands in the middle of the sea.

Portugal

Joana, the princess who chose

In Portugal, more than five hundred years ago, lived Princess Joana, daughter of King Afonso. When her father and brother went off to a war across the sea, she was the one who looked after the kingdom, at about nineteen years old.

Many kings and princes from other countries wanted to marry her, and Joana said no, one after another. She wanted to choose her own life. She went to live in a convent in the town of Aveiro, where she studied, prayed and helped people in need. Even today, the people there call her Saint Joana the Princess.

Angola

Nzinga, the princess who wouldn't sit on the floor

In Africa, where Angola is today, Princess Nzinga was born in the kingdom of Ndongo. From when she was little, she learned to fight, to negotiate and to speak other languages.

One day she went to talk with the Portuguese governor, who wanted to take her people's land. The story goes that there was only a chair for him in the room, and Nzinga was supposed to stay on the floor. Do you know what happened? One of her helpers knelt down and became a bench, and Nzinga sat at the same height as the governor, as equals.

Later she became queen of Ndongo and Matamba and defended her people for almost forty years. Lots of things in Brazil come from that region, even everyday words like cafuné and caçula.

Congo and Brazil

Aqualtune, the princess who reached Palmares

Tradition says that Aqualtune was a princess of the kingdom of Congo who led warriors. One day she was captured and brought to Brazil by force, on a ship.

But nobody could put out her courage. She escaped and went to Palmares, the biggest quilombo in Brazil, a refuge hidden high in the mountains where people lived free. There she helped lead the people, and tradition says she was the grandmother of Zumbi dos Palmares. Every time someone says her name, her story stays alive.

Nigeria

Amina, the warrior princess of Zazzau

In the north of Nigeria, about five hundred years ago, lived Princess Amina, of the kingdom of Zazzau, of the Hausa people. From when she was little, she learned to ride horses and command soldiers.

When she became queen, she led a huge army and had earth walls built around the cities to protect the people. Even today there are walls in Nigeria that people call Amina's walls. Remember the walls of Óbidos? On the other side of the sea there are warrior princess walls too!

Minas Gerais

The queens of Congado, in my homeland

Did you know that in my homeland, Minas Gerais, there are real queens even today? At the Congado festivals, Black communities crown a king and a queen of Congo. The queen parades down the street with a crown, an embroidered cloak and a sceptre, and the groups go ahead singing, dancing and beating drums. Sometimes there's even a child prince and princess in the procession.

This festival remembers the kings and queens of the old kingdom of Congo, in Africa, and it's also a celebration for Our Lady of the Rosary. Often the queen is an older lady, deeply respected, who looks after the tradition and teaches it to the children. To me, they're the most beautiful queens there are.

Korea

Pyeonggang, the princess who believed in Ondal

In Korea, the land of K-pop, people tell the story of Princess Pyeonggang, of an ancient kingdom called Goguryeo. When she was little she cried a lot, and the king, her father, joked: if you don't stop crying, I'll marry you to Ondal the Fool! Ondal was a very poor young man everyone thought was foolish.

When the princess grew up, the king wanted her to marry a rich nobleman. And she answered: you promised Ondal, and a king doesn't go back on his word. She left the palace, went to find Ondal and taught him everything she knew: reading, writing, riding and fighting. She sold her own jewels to buy him a horse. And Ondal, whom everyone called a fool, became the most famous general in the kingdom.

Japan

Kaguya, the princess from the Moon

In Japan there's a tale more than a thousand years old. An old bamboo cutter saw a shining bamboo stalk and, inside it, a tiny girl no bigger than a finger. He and his wife raised her, and she grew up to be the most beautiful and the cleverest young woman in Japan: Princess Kaguya.

Five princes wanted to marry her. She asked each of them to bring her an impossible treasure, like the skin of a fire rat and the jewel from a dragon's neck. None of them managed it, some tried to trick her, and she caught them all. In the end she told her secret: she came from the Moon! On a night with a full moon, the people of the Moon came to fetch her. When the moon is nice and round, give Kaguya a little wave.

Hawaii

Kaʻiulani, the princess of the islands

In the middle of the Pacific Ocean are the islands of Hawaii, which were once a kingdom with Hawaiian kings and queens. Princess Kaʻiulani was born there about a hundred and fifty years ago. She loved surfing, riding horses and painting.

While she was studying far away, in England, some rich men took the government away from the queen of Hawaii, her aunt, with the help of United States soldiers. Kaʻiulani was only seventeen and didn't stay quiet: she crossed the ocean, went to the United States and spoke to journalists and to the president to defend her people. She couldn't save the kingdom, but the Hawaiian people still remember her with great love.

For grown-ups: what is history and what is legend

  • Joana of Portugal (1452-1490), daughter of Afonso V: regent in 1471, during the Arzila campaign; she entered the Convent of Jesus in Aveiro. Beatified in 1693. "Saint Joana the Princess" is the popular name; she was never canonised.
  • Nzinga Mbande (c. 1583-1663): the chair scene, in Luanda in 1622, comes from 17th-century missionary accounts, above all Cavazzi's. It is widely quoted and has a layer of legend.
  • Aqualtune: the records are very thin and almost everything comes from oral tradition and later texts. That's why the story says "tradition says".
  • Amina of Zazzau (16th century): the main written source is from the 19th century, together with oral tradition. The walls called ganuwar Amina exist; the attribution is tradition.
  • Congado or Reinado: the crowning of Congo kings and queens, with devotion to Our Lady of the Rosary. Its form varies a lot from one community to another.
  • Pyeonggang and Ondal: a legend recorded in the Samguk Sagi (1145). Ondal may have been a real 6th-century general.
  • Kaguya: the Taketori Monogatari, a Japanese tale from the 9th or 10th century.
  • Kaʻiulani (1875-1899): in 1893 she went to the United States to oppose the overthrow of Queen Liliʻuokalani and met President Cleveland.

Odds or evens?

A hand game played in many countries, and the journey of numbers all the way to your hand, in short stories.

What odd and even mean

Odds or evens? I pick odds! Do you know how to tell if a number is even? Just try making pairs. If everyone has someone to hold hands with, the number is even. If someone is left without a partner, it's odd. Four friends make two pairs and nobody is left over: four is even. Five friends make two pairs and one is left over: five is odd. Two, four, six, eight and ten are even. One, three, five, seven and nine are odd. And zero? Zero is even too!

The secret of the hand game

In the hand game, each player shows some fingers, you add them all up and count. And there's a detective's secret: if both players show an even number, or both show an odd number, the total is even. If one shows even and the other odd, the total is odd. Try it with someone at home!

The journey of numbers

The numbers born in India

Now look at the numbers we use for counting: one, two, three, up to nine, and zero. Do you know where they come from? They were born in India, more than fifteen hundred years ago. Then Arab and Persian scholars learned them, used them a lot and carried them to other places, all the way to Europe. That's why they're called Hindu-Arabic numerals. And here's a lovely thing: Arabs call these numbers Indian, because they learned them from India, and Europeans called them Arabic, because they learned them from the Arabs. Each people remembering who taught them.

The Roman numerals on your seals

Before that, in Europe, people used Roman numerals, made with letters, like I, V and X. They were invented by the ancient Romans, in Italy! Remember the seals in your mission? They have Roman numerals! Try adding eighteen and four written like this: XVIII plus IV. Tricky, right? With Hindu-Arabic numerals it's easy: eighteen plus four, twenty-two. And there's one number the Romans didn't have at all: zero. Without zero, you can't write ten, a hundred and a thousand the way we write them.

The scholar of the House of Wisdom

In Baghdad, a city that is in Iraq today, there was the House of Wisdom, a huge library where scholars from many peoples translated books from Greece, India and Persia. One of these scholars, who came from Persia, was called al-Khwarizmi. He wrote a book teaching how to do sums with these numbers, and the book became so famous that his name turned into a word: algorithm comes from his name! And the word algebra comes from the title of another book he wrote.

The abacus, the bead calculator

Long before calculators existed, people were already doing big sums. Do you know how? With little pebbles! In ancient Rome, merchants moved pebbles on a board to count. In Latin, a little pebble was called calculus. That's why, even today, doing sums is called calculating!

Then the pebbles became beads on rods: the abacus. Many peoples had their own. In China there's the suanpan, in Japan there's the soroban and in Russia there's the schoty. In Japan, lots of children still learn the soroban today, and the most practised ones calculate so fast that they picture the abacus in their head and do sums without it!

On my abacus, each rod has a job. The one on the right counts the ones, the one in the middle counts the tens and the one on the left counts the hundreds. When the ones rod gets to ten beads, they turn into just one bead on the tens rod. Shall we practise?

The Arabs and the stars

And the stars? Arab astronomers studied the sky every night. They perfected the astrolabe, a metal disc that helps you know the time and the position of the stars. Lots of stars still have Arabic names today, like Aldebaran, Altair and Vega. Portuguese sailors used the astrolabe too, to find their way in the middle of the sea by looking at the sky.

So next time you play odds or evens, remember: every number you count has made an enormous journey, from India, through the Arabs, all the way to your hand.

Photography: writing with light

Where photos come from, how a camera works, how to photograph people and the stories of brave women photographers.Short stories on the origins of photography, the shutter, the ethics of photographing people and women photographers who opened the way, with a shutter-speed game.

The film camera

Have you ever seen a film camera? It's like the ones everybody used before phones. One of the most famous is called the Pentax K1000, and it's so simple that it takes photos even without a battery: the battery is only for the needle that measures the light.

And do you know what photography means? The word comes from Greek: photo means light, and graphy means writing. Taking a photo is writing with light! Come and find out how it all began.

Where photos come from

The dark room

Long before cameras existed, people discovered a kind of magic. If you make a room really dark and poke a tiny hole in the window, the light comes in through the hole and draws the view outside on the wall. But upside down! It's called a camera obscura, and camera means room. That's where the word camera comes from.

In China, the scholar Mozi wrote about it more than two thousand years ago. And about a thousand years ago, an Arab scholar called Ibn al-Haytham did lots of experiments with the camera obscura and explained the secret: light travels in straight lines. Remember the Arab scholars from odds or evens? They were everywhere! With a grown-up, you can make a camera obscura from a shoebox, a tiny hole on one side and a sheet of tracing paper on the other.

The first photo in the world

The camera obscura showed the picture, but it didn't keep it. As soon as you closed the hole, the picture disappeared. In France, an inventor called Nicéphore Niépce found a way: he spread a coating made from a kind of asphalt on a metal plate, and it hardens wherever light hits it.

In 1827 he put the plate inside a camera, pointed at the window of his house, and waited. And waited. And waited! At least eight hours, and some people think it took days. The photo shows the rooftops outside, and it's the oldest photo that still exists. Good thing it only takes one click now, right? But the click needs your attention, because the moment goes by fast and doesn't come back.

An inventor in Brazil

Now for a surprise: photography was invented in Brazil too! A Frenchman called Hercule Florence moved to Brazil when he was young. He travelled as far as the Amazon, drawing plants, animals and Indigenous peoples, and then went to live in Campinas, near São Paulo.

In 1833, together with a friend who knew about chemistry, he experimented with light and a silver salt, and managed to copy drawings and pharmacy labels. He gave his invention the name photographie, French for photography, before almost anyone else! But hardly anybody found out. More than a hundred years later, a Brazilian researcher, Boris Kossoy, found his notebooks and told the world.

The secret of film

And inside a film camera, what's there? A roll of film: a little rolled-up strip covered in grains made with silver, so tiny you can't even see them. When you press the button, the light comes in, touches the grains and they change. The photo is already there, but it's still invisible!

For the photo to appear, the film has to be developed in a dark place, with special liquids. That's why nobody opens the camera in the light with film inside: the light rushes in all at once and ruins everything. When the roll is finished, you turn a little crank to wind the strip back into its canister, and only then do you open the camera. The photos stay in there, asleep, until someone develops them. What a surprise!

How a camera works

The shutter's little door

Look at the front of the camera: that's the lens, which is like a glass eye. Behind it, hidden away, there's a little cloth door that's always closed, so light can't reach the film. It's called the shutter. When you press the button, clack! The little door opens and closes really fast.

And you choose the speed, by turning the little dial on top of the camera. On the Pentax, the door can stay open for a whole second, or for just one thousandth of a second, much faster than a blink! Really fast, only a little light gets in, but the photo can freeze even a seagull in flight. Really slow, lots of light gets in, but anything that moves comes out blurry. And inside the viewfinder lives a little needle: when it sits in the middle, the light is just right.

Photographing people

First, ask permission

A photo keeps a little piece of a person: their face, their way of being, a moment of their life. That's why, before photographing someone, you ask permission. Can I take your photo? If the person says no, that's fine: you lower the camera and smile. If it's a child, you ask their family too.

And there are sacred places and moments that aren't photographed. In many churches, mosques, temples and candomblé houses, the people who look after the place decide. Photographing with respect means treating the person in the photo the way you'd like to be treated. And afterwards, why not show them the photo?

What's left out of the photo

Notice something: a photo shows only a little piece of the world, the piece the photographer chose. Everything outside the frame, we can't see. If I take a photo of just your scraped knee, whoever looks at it might think you're always getting hurt. But you also jump, dance, sing and win at odds or evens!

So when you look at a photo, ask three detective questions. Who took this photo? Did the person in the photo agree? And what was left out?

The story of a single photo

Now for a serious talk. For a long time, many white people from rich countries have travelled to Africa and photographed African children. Often they don't ask the child's name, don't ask the family's permission and choose only the sad photos, of children crying or in torn clothes. Then they show these photos to ask for money, or to look like heroes.

Whoever sees only these photos thinks all of Africa is like that. But Africa has 54 countries, enormous cities, schools, scientists, musicians, festivals and lots and lots of children playing, just like you. A Nigerian writer, Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie, calls this the danger of a single story: when we know just one story about a people, we think that people is only that. Every child deserves the same respect you do: to have a name, to be asked and to be shown whole.

Radiators for Norway

Some students from Norway, a freezing cold country in the north of Europe, came up with a joke to show how strange this is. In 2012 they made a pretend video, Radi-Aid, where a group of African singers collects radiators to send to the children of Norway, poor things, freezing to death!

Everyone laughed, because Norway has much more than cold. And that's when lots of people understood: it's the same with Africa, it has much more than those sad photos. Today many African photographers show their people as they really are. Want to meet one of them?

Women photographers who opened the way

Ghana

Felicia Abban, from Ghana

For a long time, many people thought photography was a job for men. Some brave women showed them they were wrong. In Ghana, in Africa, Felicia Abban's father was a photographer, and she started learning from him at fourteen. At eighteen, she opened her own studio in Accra, the capital, and gave it a lovely name: the Day and Night studio. She was Ghana's first woman professional photographer.

People came to the studio in their best clothes, and Felicia photographed each of them the way they wanted to be seen. And she had a habit I love: before going out to a party, she'd get dressed up and take a photo of herself. She also photographed Ghana's first president. Many years later, her photos went to the Venice Biennale, a big art exhibition in Italy.

India

Homai Vyarawalla, from India

In India, almost a hundred years ago, Homai Vyarawalla walked the streets of Delhi in a sari with a camera in her hand. For a long time she was the only woman newspaper photographer in the country. At first, her photos appeared in the paper under her husband's name! Then she invented a photographer's name all her own: Dalda 13.

The men photographers thought she was just a girl playing at taking photos, and didn't even notice her. Do you know what she did? She made the most of it, got up close and took the best photos! She photographed the time when India was fighting to be free, the country's leaders and the people in the streets. When she was very old, she received one of India's most important awards.

Mexico

Graciela Iturbide and the iguana lady

In Mexico, the photographer Graciela Iturbide was invited to visit Juchitán, a town where Zapotec women run the market. They sell cloth, tomatoes and bread, and carry everything balanced on their heads.

In 1979, Graciela saw a woman named Zobeida with iguanas on her head, like a crown! She asked if she could photograph her, and Zobeida said yes. The photo became famous all over the world. Years later, someone asked Zobeida if she was a feminist. She said yes, because she worked and took care of herself. And Graciela went back to Juchitán many times, for almost ten years, to get to know those women well.

Brazil

Lita Cerqueira, from Bahia

In Bahia, in Brazil, there's a photographer I really admire: Lita Cerqueira. She taught herself photography when she lived in Rio de Janeiro, taking photos of her son and the children playing in a square. The children's parents liked them so much that they bought the photos!

She's considered the first Black woman photographer from Bahia. For more than fifty years she has photographed the Black people of Bahia: street festivals, capoeira, the women of the Sisterhood of Good Death and musicians like Gilberto Gil and Gal Costa. One of her exhibitions was called Black People Are My People. In Lita's photos, we look beautiful, joyful and whole.

United States

Vivian Maier, the nanny photographer

In the United States there lived a nanny called Vivian Maier. For more than forty years she looked after children, always with a camera hanging round her neck. While she was out walking with the children, she photographed the streets, the people and even herself, reflected in shop windows, or her own shadow on the pavement.

She took more than a hundred thousand photos and hardly showed them to anyone. Lots of rolls of film she never even developed! In 2007, a young man bought a box of her negatives at an auction and was amazed: they were wonderful photos. Today they're in museums. And those rolls of film, left undeveloped for years, were quietly keeping a treasure.

Now it's your turn

Here are my secrets for taking photos. Hold the camera steady with both hands and tuck your elbows against your tummy, so it doesn't shake. Notice where the light is coming from. Ask permission before photographing someone. And pay close attention, because the beautiful moment goes by in a flash: whoever is quick and watchful gets the photo!

When you have some photos of your own, pick your favourite and tell someone what was happening at that moment.
